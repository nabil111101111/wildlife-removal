Wildlife Damage and Homeowners Insurance

The short answer: insurers often treat the damage an animal did differently from the cost of removing the animal, and many policies exclude rodents outright. The long answer, with the documentation habits that decide claims, is this page.

The distinction that decides most claims

Policies generally insure your house against damage. An animal is not a covered event in itself; it is a cause. So the removal bill, the operator's charge to evict a raccoon and seal the soffit, is usually treated as the homeowner's maintenance cost, while the harm the animal did, torn ducting, ruined insulation, chewed wiring repaired by an electrician, sometimes even a damaged roof section, may be covered as property damage depending on the policy's wording on animals. Two invoices from the same week can meet two different answers, and that split surprises almost everyone who meets it for the first time.

The second filter is the species. Many standard policies exclude damage by rodents, and often insects and vermin, by name. A raccoon or a bat colony can sit on the covered side of that line in one policy while mice sit on the excluded side of the same page. Squirrels, being rodents, land in the argument zone, which is why the exact policy wording and the operator's report language matter. Read your own policy's animal wording before you need it; it is four lines in most documents and it settles most of the question.

  • Removal cost: usually the homeowner's bill
  • Damage repair: sometimes covered, policy by policy
  • Rodent damage: excluded in many standard policies

How to document wildlife damage for a claim

Claims live on paper. Keep the inspection report with its dated photographs of entry points and damage, the itemised quote separating removal from repair work, the cleanup note describing contaminated zones removed, and invoices that match. Photograph damage before it is repaired where safety allows, including chewed wiring before the electrician replaces it and insulation zones before they are bagged. Note dates: when noise started, when damage was found, when the insurer was told. A claim reported late, after a full restoration, asks the insurer to pay for damage it can no longer see, and adjusters price what they can verify.

Call early and ask narrow questions: is animal damage covered under my policy, which animals are excluded, does the claim affect my premium or my no-claims position, and is the amount above my deductible enough to be worth claiming at all. For a modest insulation zone the honest answer may be to pay and keep the record clean. For wiring, roof damage and wide contamination, the claim may be the difference between full restoration and a patch. The operator's ranges help you judge: single-animal removal $300-$600 is usually below the point of claiming, while damage running alongside bat exclusion at $800-$2,500 changes the sums.

Special cases: landlords, second homes and wiring

Landlord policies and second-home policies carry their own animal and vacancy clauses, and a house left empty for winter can complicate a claim if the insurer argues the damage ran undiscovered for months. The audience pages on this site deal with those cases in their own terms. Wiring deserves a mention here: where an animal's chewing leads to a fire or an electrical failure, the resulting event can be treated differently from the chewing itself. That distinction is policy-specific and fact-specific, which is a long way of saying: document the chewing when you find it, repair it promptly for safety, and let the paperwork argue the coverage question afterwards.

Frequently asked questions about insurance

Is wildlife removal covered by homeowners insurance?

The removal itself, usually not. Insurers treat evicting an animal and sealing its entry as home maintenance, like clearing a blocked drain. The damage the animal caused is the part that can be covered, depending on your policy. So expect to pay the operator's removal bill yourself, inspection $150-$300 and single-animal removal $300-$600, and to claim, if anywhere, on the repair side: insulation, wiring, ducting and roof damage the inspection documented. Does insurance cover raccoon damage to an attic?

Often it can, because raccoons are not rodents and many policies cover damage by larger wildlife while excluding rodents and insects. The latrine cleanup, ruined insulation and torn materials sit on the damage side of the line. Your policy wording decides, so read its animal clause and call with the inspection report in hand. Photographs of the latrine and the zones removed, with dates, are what turn a phone argument into a processed claim. Are you covered for bat guano cleanup?

Cleanup of contamination is damage remediation in most readings, which puts it closer to the covered side than the exclusion bill itself. Policies vary, and gradual damage discovered late can be contested, which is why an early call with a dated inspection report matters. The report should separate the three figures cleanly: exclusion $800-$2,500, cleanup as quoted after inspection, and any insulation restoration. An insurer who can see the split can answer each part on its own terms. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Is wiring damage by squirrels covered?

This is the contested corner. Squirrels are rodents, and rodent exclusions appear in many policies, so the chewed cable itself may be excluded. Where chewing leads to a further event, cover can be argued differently. None of that changes the safety sequence: find it, photograph it, have an electrician repair it promptly. Then pursue the claim with the operator's report and the electrician's invoice, and accept that this category is the one most likely to end in a policy-wording answer. Does insurance cover roof damage caused by raccoons?

Roof damage, torn shingles, lifted flashing and a wrecked vent, is property damage in the plain sense, and it stands a better chance than the removal bill, subject to your policy's animal wording and your deductible. The operator's before photographs of the entry point, showing animal damage rather than wear, are the evidence that matters. Keep the roofing or exclusion invoice itemised so the repair element is visible as itself, not folded into a single removal total. What is the difference between removal cost and damage repair for insurance?

Removal cost is what it takes to get the animal out and keep it out: inspection, eviction and exclusion. Damage repair is what it takes to put the house back: insulation replaced, wiring repaired, ducts and materials restored, contamination cleaned. Insurers draw the line because one is maintenance and the other is loss. Your paperwork should draw the same line before they ask, with separate figures and separate invoices or invoice sections for each side. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

How should I photograph wildlife damage for a claim?

Photograph wide, then close. Wide shots place the damage in the attic or roof; close shots show teeth marks, droppings, staining and stripped cable in a way that cannot be confused with age or wear. Include something for scale, a ruler or a coin, in close work. Keep the originals with their dates intact, and add the operator's photographs from inspection, which carry the credibility of a third party who found the damage before anyone mentioned a claim. My claim for animal damage was denied. What next?

Ask for the denial in writing with the policy clause relied on, then check it against your documents: was the species one the policy excludes, was the damage classed as gradual, was the deductible the real reason? If the paperwork misstates the animal or the damage, correct it with the operator's report and photographs and ask for a review. A broker or agent who knows your policy can tell you quickly whether the denial is a wording wall or a paperwork gap worth one more attempt. Does a landlord's insurance cover wildlife damage in a rental?

Landlord policies commonly cover the building's damage on similar animal terms, while the removal bill sits with whoever the lease and local practice assign it to, covered on the landlords page here. Vacancy and discovery clauses bite harder in rentals, so a documented inspection rhythm is worth more, not less. Keep the operator reports per property and per visit, because a claim on a multi-unit building lives or dies on which unit, which date and which animal the file can show. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Should I claim for a small amount of wildlife damage?

Run the arithmetic first: the amount above your deductible, set against any premium effect of claiming, which your insurer or broker can outline. A single-zone insulation repair may sit below the sensible claim line. Wide contamination, wiring and roof work usually sit above it. Either way, keep the full documentation. A homeowner who pays quietly for a small claim still owns a dated file that proves the house was found, fixed and sealed, which serves at sale time even when no insurer was involved. Will insurance pay for preventive exclusion before any damage?

Almost never. Prevention is maintenance in every policy's eyes, like repointing or gutter clearing, and it is bought for its own return: the damage that never happens. Its economics stand without an insurer. A September sealing pass costs a fraction of one occupied winter's cleanup and repairs. File prevention under the same heading as a boiler service: unglamorous, unrecoverable from any policy, and the reason the expensive pages of this site stay unread in most years.
