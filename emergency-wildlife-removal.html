Emergency Wildlife Removal: An Animal Inside Tonight

An animal in the living space is a different problem from an animal in the attic. It needs calm steps tonight, not a full removal plan. This page gives the tonight protocol for bats, raccoons and other animals indoors, and explains what an emergency visit does.

The bat in the house tonight protocol

If a bat is flying in a room, stay calm and keep people and pets out of that room. Open an exterior window or door in the room if you can do it without going near the bat, and close the doors to the rest of the house. Many bats leave on their own through the open window once the room is quiet and dark. Place a towel along the gap under the closed door so the bat cannot slip into the hallway. Do not chase it with a broom or try to catch it in flight.

One rule overrides the rest. If the bat was in a room with a sleeping person, a child, or anyone who cannot say for sure whether contact happened, do not release it until you have checked health guidance. Keep the bat contained in that room and contact your county health department or health professionals for advice, and a wildlife operator who can capture the bat safely. That single decision, made tonight, is the one that matters most on this page.

Raccoons and other animals in living space

A raccoon that comes down a chimney or through a pet door wants an exit, not a fight. Confine it to one room by closing other doors, open an outside door from that room, and leave. Most animals take the exit within an hour once the house is quiet. Keep dogs and cats locked away: a cornered raccoon can injure a pet badly, and a bite to a person turns a removal job into a health matter. Never corner the animal yourself, and never block its only exit while standing in the room.

A skunk indoors is the case for patience above all. Noise and rushing cause spraying. Darken the room, open an outside door, lay a quiet trail away from people, and give it time. If any animal will not leave, seems injured, or is in a room you cannot seal off, that is the point of an emergency callout: an operator with catch equipment and a plan for the entry point, tonight rather than at a routine visit.

What an emergency visit covers

An emergency visit solves tonight first: the animal is captured or guided out safely, any immediate health question is flagged with the right guidance to check, and the room is made secure. Finding and sealing the entry route follows, sometimes the same visit, sometimes as a booked follow-up in daylight, when rooflines can be inspected properly. Be wary of any service that removes the animal and leaves without discussing how it entered. A bat through an open window is chance. A bat through a gable vent is a colony waiting upstairs.

How this service fits the wider job

Removal on its own rarely ends a wildlife problem in upstate New York. Animals follow scent trails, roof gaps and easy food, and a house that suited one raccoon or squirrel will suit the next one. That is why emergency wildlife removal sits inside a wider sequence: inspect the whole roofline and attic, remove the animals that are present, seal every entry point with materials animals cannot chew through, then clean up what was left behind. Skip a step and the odds of a repeat visit climb fast. Homeowners in Ithaca, Binghamton, Poughkeepsie and the other towns we cover see the same pattern each winter, when cold weather pushes animals toward warm attic space between October and March.

Bat in the house tonight: the protocol

1. Isolate the room

Get people and pets out and close the door so the bat stays in one known space.

2. Block the door gap

Lay a towel along the bottom of the door so the bat cannot slip into the hall.

3. Open a way out

Open an exterior window or door in that room and leave the room quiet and dark.

4. Decide on capture, not release

If anyone slept in the room, or a child or unattended person was present, keep the bat contained and check health guidance before any release.

5. Call for help

Contact your county health department for health guidance and a licensed operator for safe capture and an entry-point check.

Frequently asked questions about emergency removal

A bat is in my house right now. What do I do first?

Isolate the bat in one room with people and pets outside it. Close the door, lay a towel along the gap underneath, and open an exterior window in that room. Leave the room quiet. Many bats find the window and leave within the hour. If the bat settles, or if anyone was sleeping in that room, move to capture and health guidance rather than release. For health questions, consult health professionals or your county health department and follow current NYSDOH guidance for your situation.

Should I try to catch a bat myself?

Only if it has landed, you have thick gloves and a rigid container, and no one slept in the room. Slide the container over the bat, slip card underneath, and keep it contained for advice. Never handle a bat bare-handed, and never attempt to catch one in flight. If any of those conditions is missing, an operator with proper equipment is the safer and usually quicker option. A raccoon came down my chimney into the fireplace. What now?

Keep the damper or doors closed if you can, keep pets away from that room, and close the room off. Do not open the fireplace doors to look. An operator can fit a one-way route or capture the animal from the fireplace safely, then cap the chimney so it cannot repeat. If the animal is already loose in the room, open an outside door, leave, and let it choose the exit. This page gives general information only. Can a skunk be removed from inside a house without spraying?

Often, yes, with patience. Most indoor skunk removals succeed by giving the animal a dark, quiet exit route rather than by capture. Operators may use a low, enclosed trap for a skunk that will not leave. Spraying is a last resort for the animal, and calm handling avoids it in most cases. If a spray does happen indoors, that room needs odour treatment after the animal is out. There is an animal in my wall tonight, scratching. Is that an emergency?

Scratching in a wall at night is urgent for your sleep but rarely an emergency for safety. Do not cut the wall yourself and do not block any outside hole. Note the times and the room, keep pets away, and book an inspection for the morning. It becomes an emergency if the animal breaks into living space, if a bat is involved with a sleeping person, or if you smell gas, burning or see electrical problems near the noise. What if the animal is injured or acting strangely?

Keep well away and keep pets inside. An animal that staggers, circles, approaches people in daylight or seems unafraid may be ill, and it should be reported rather than approached. Your county health department or a licensed operator can advise on the right response for the species and the circumstances. Do not offer food or water, and do not let children watch from close range, however calm the animal appears. This page gives general information only. Will an emergency visit also seal how the animal got in?

The entry point is identified on the emergency visit where light and access allow, and sealing is either completed or booked as the immediate follow-up. Temporary measures, a screened vent or a secured chimney, can be fitted on the night. Full exclusion in daylight is safer and more thorough than ladder work at midnight, and it should be confirmed in writing before the operator leaves. Does animal control handle emergency wildlife in the house?

Municipal animal control usually covers domestic animals and animals at large outdoors. A wild animal inside your home, in a wall or in a chimney is normally the work of a licensed nuisance wildlife control operator. If there is immediate danger to people, or an animal acting in a way that suggests rabies, your county health department and local authorities can advise on the fastest route. Otherwise, the operator route gets you capture plus the entry-point fix in one visit. For health questions, consult health professionals or your county health department and follow current NYSDOH guidance for your situation.

My dog cornered an animal in the yard at night. What should I check?

Call the dog in and keep it leashed until the animal has gone. Check your dog for bites or scratches once inside, and note what the animal was if you saw it. Any bite from a wild animal to a pet is a matter for your veterinarian promptly, and for health guidance where rabies is a question. Do not let the dog back out to investigate, and check fences and gates in daylight for the gap that was used. This page gives general information only.
