Questions, grouped by topic

Speed and emergencies

How fast can you get a wildlife removal technician to my home?

Routine inspections are booked in days; animals in living space and bats in bedrooms jump the queue for same-day or same-night help where an operator is on call. The honest answer is a window, not a minute count, because the operator may be on a roof two towns away. While you wait, the emergency page keeps the situation stable with a closed room and a towel under the door.

There's a raccoon in my attic. What should I do right now?

Keep the hatch closed, keep pets away from the attic side of the house, and do not block any hole you know about. Note the times you hear it and photograph droppings from the hatch in daylight if that is safe. Then book an inspection. A raccoon at night is a routine call handled over days with one-way doors and a babies check, not a midnight emergency, unless it breaks into living space.

Do you offer emergency or same-day wildlife removal?

Yes for the cases that need it: a bat in the house, an animal in a room, a skunk indoors. Planned cases, attic residents, deck dens, colonies, are booked as inspections and handled properly over the days the method needs. Same-day promises for every problem usually mean steps skipped. Ask any service which cases it treats as emergencies and judge the answer; it reveals the whole operation.

How long does wildlife removal take once it starts?

Most jobs run over several nights because one-way doors work on the animals' hours. Squirrels and flying squirrels often clear inside a week. A raccoon family takes a planned sequence around the young. Bat exclusion follows the legal windows. Exclusion usually fits in a day once the structure is empty, and cleanup adds time by zone. The inspection gives you the schedule for your house, in writing, with the quote.

Cost and value

How much does wildlife removal cost?

Four blocks cover most jobs: inspection $150-$300, a single-animal removal $300-$600 with basic sealing, bat exclusion $800-$2,500, and cleanup quoted after inspection from the zone map. A simple squirrel eviction lands near the bottom of that system. A raccoon family with a latrine, full exclusion and cleanup lands higher, stage by stage. The pricing hub explains what moves each figure and the questions to ask before signing.

Why do quotes vary so much between companies?

Because quotes hide different jobs under one name. One includes removal, sealing in metal, cleanup and a written warranty. Another is a single trap visit. Roof access, entry points, contamination and young in season move honest prices too. Compare itemised quotes stage by stage, with photographs behind the findings, against the ranges on the pricing hub. The variation shrinks to sense once you can see what each price actually buys.

Is wildlife removal covered by homeowners insurance?

The removal bill usually is not; the damage sometimes is. Policies often exclude rodents by name, treat larger wildlife differently, and separate the eviction cost from repairs to insulation, wiring and roofing. Photograph everything, keep the dated inspection report, split removal and repair figures on the paperwork, and call the insurer early. The insurance guide on this site walks the distinction and the claim file in full.

Do you offer free inspections?

Inspections here run $150-$300 and are often credited toward the removal work. A genuine inspection is a roofline walk, attic time and photographs, and a service giving it away usually recovers the cost inside the job price. The fair question is not whether the inspection is free but whether it is credited, and whether you receive the findings and photographs either way. Get both answers with the booking, in writing.

Humane methods and poison

Do you use poison for animals in the attic?

No. Poison is refused on every matched job, mice and rats included. Poisoned animals die in walls and soffits, pets and birds of prey eat poisoned animals and carrion, and no hole gets sealed by any of it. The method here is eviction or lawful trapping, then metal exclusion, then cleanup. That order solves the animal, the access and the aftermath in one job, which poison never attempts.

Are your methods humane?

Yes, by a published standard rather than a slogan. One-way doors let animals leave under their own power. Mothers and young are kept together under a written protocol. Glue traps and body-gripping traps are not used in homes. Relocation follows New York law, which mostly means resolving the problem on site. The humane standard page states all of it, so any operator can be measured against it after the job as well as before.

What animals do you remove?

Raccoons, bats, grey and flying squirrels, skunks, groundhogs, opossums, mice and rats in structures, and birds in chimneys and vents. Each has its own page here with signs, seasons and costs. The service is wildlife only: no insect spraying, no subscriptions. If your problem turns out to be insects, a pest control firm is the right call and the inspection will say so plainly.

What if there are baby animals in my attic?

The job switches to the mother-and-babies protocol. The litter is found and aged, the mother is given an eviction route, the young are placed in a warm reunion box by the exit for her to collect, and sealing waits until the whole family is confirmed out. It adds days in most cases. Separating a mother from her young, or sealing young inside, is refused on matched jobs without exception.

Sealing and exclusion

Will you seal the entry points or just remove the animal?

Both, in that order, because each half fails alone. Removal without sealing leaves the hole, the scent and the invitation, and the next animal arrives within a season. Sealing without removal traps the current animal inside. A proper quote lists the points found at inspection and the material fitted to each: quarter-inch hardware cloth, flashing, caps and guards. Photographs before and after are part of the deliverable.

What does exclusion mean, and why is it important?

Exclusion means closing every route an animal uses or could use, with materials it cannot chew or pull through, once the structure is empty. It is important because it is the only part of the job that speaks to next year. Animals return to proven dens by scent and memory. A sealed house fails their test on the first night, and they move to softer targets. The exclusion hub page covers materials and warranty framing in full.

How long does exclusion work last?

Metal exclusion fitted well should last for years, often the life of the vent, cap or roof section it protects. Its enemies are the house around it: storm damage, new roof work and rotting fascia opening fresh gaps. An annual roofline walk each September catches those changes while they are small. Warranty terms for sealed points come from your operator in writing, and the photographs from the job show exactly what was fitted where.

Can I seal the house myself?

Ground-level sealing on an empty structure, yes, with the prevention guide open beside you. Roofline sealing at height, and any sealing where animals, young or a colony may still be inside, belongs to an operator. The common DIY failure is sealing the used hole while a second point stands open, or sealing an animal in. An inspection first, with its mapped list of points, is what makes any sealing, yours or an operator's, complete.

Cleanup and damage

Do you clean up droppings and contaminated insulation?

Yes. Cleanup is a stage of the job, scoped from a zone map at inspection: droppings and nesting removed, contaminated insulation bagged out by zone, surfaces treated, and insulation restored where it was lost. Light scatter and a deep bat attic are different jobs and are priced as such, after inspection, never guessed from the doorstep. The attic cleanup page explains the levels and the methods in detail.

Is bat guano dangerous to clean myself?

Small amounts, handled damp with gloves and a mask, sit within careful DIY reach. Deep accumulations under a long-used roost do not: disturbed dry guano loads the air with dust that can carry histoplasmosis, and the work needs respirators and HEPA equipment. Raccoon latrines are professional work for the same class of reasons. The droppings page draws the line by source and scale; if your attic sits past it, believe the page.

Can animals damage my wiring, insulation or roof?

Yes, and these are the costs that dwarf the removal bill when they run. Squirrels and rodents strip cable insulation, a genuine fire risk. Raccoons tear shingles, soffits and vents and ruin insulation around a latrine. Bat guano loads and stains insulation under a roost for years. The inspection checks all three and photographs what it finds, which is also the file an insurance claim will later stand on.

Will the smell go away after removal?

Smell lives in sources: droppings, urine-soaked insulation, a latrine, or a carcass. Remove the animal and the smell fades as the source is cleaned or removed in the cleanup stage. Masking products over a live source fail within weeks. If odour persists after a full cleanup, an unfound source remains, usually in a wall void or soffit, and the operator should hunt it rather than re-spray it.

Licensing and trust

Are wildlife removal providers licensed and insured in New York?

They should be, under the nuisance wildlife control operator licence issued by the Department of Environmental Conservation, with insurance for the building work. Licensed and insured is a claim you can check: ask for the licence name, verify it with the DEC, and ask for the insurer's details on a large job. The verification page walks through the whole check in minutes. Matched operators here are held to exactly that standard.

How do I know a wildlife website is genuine?

Look for a named owner, licence details, consistent prices across the site, and town pages with real local detail rather than the same paragraphs with the town swapped. Be wary of testimonials without surnames or platforms, and of any site that admits its reviews are illustrative. Our own research found pages written for other states ranking in New York searches. Three questions about your town, answered without pausing, separate the genuine article fastest.

Do you offer a warranty on wildlife work?

Exclusion carries the matched operator's written warranty on the points sealed: the points, the period and the remedy if an animal re-enters through one of them, all in your paperwork before work starts. A warranty cannot honestly cover new damage elsewhere on the house, and any firm promising unlimited cover in vague words should be asked to write it down. The verification page lists the warranty among the questions to ask any operator.

Babies, seasons and timing

When is the worst time of year for attic animals?

For the homeowner, spring: raccoon kits from March, squirrel litters, skunk kits under decks and the bat maternity blackout from May 1 all land together, and every job slows to the pace young animals set. For the animals' pressure on houses, autumn is the peak, as the October to March entry push fills attics for winter. The calendar page lays the whole year out; September sealing is the move that dodges both seasons.

Can bats be removed in summer?

Not by exclusion during the maternity blackout, May 1 to August 15, when flightless pups are in the roost. A colony found then is inspected, the house is made as safe as the situation allows, and the exclusion is booked for the window from mid-August. Any operator offering instant summer exclusion without mentioning pups is offering to break the rule that protects the colony and the law that protects you from the consequences.

What should I do about a skunk under the deck in spring?

Assume kits until proven otherwise and go slowly. A one-way door is timed to the young being mobile, or the family is given the weeks it needs, then the buried mesh barrier goes in. Do not seal the den, do not flood it and do not set mothballs anywhere near it. Keep dogs leashed at dusk meanwhile. The under-deck page gives the full sequence, including how emptiness is verified before any trench is dug.

Why do animals keep choosing my house every year?

Because it passes their three tests: a gap they can use, scent that says the den is proven, and food within a short walk. Each occupation strengthens all three. Breaking the pattern needs all three answered in one job: animals evicted, every point sealed in metal, droppings and scent cleaned, and yard food managed. Single-measure years, a trap visit here, a spray there, are how a house becomes a tradition.

Commercial and other cases

Do you handle wildlife in commercial buildings?

The pilot is residential, and commercial work, warehouses, restaurants, multi-unit blocks beyond a landlord arrangement, is quoted case by case through the landlords route or referred on. The methods do not change: inspection, humane removal, exclusion, cleanup. The paperwork grows: access hours, tenants, food safety rules in a restaurant. Ask early if your building is commercial, because scheduling around operating hours shapes the whole plan.

Does animal control remove animals from houses?

Municipal animal control covers domestic animals and wildlife at large in public places. Wildlife inside a private structure, attic, chimney, walls or under a deck, is the licensed operator's work. The exceptions are safety cases: an animal acting as if rabid, or an injured animal in a public place, where animal control and your county health department are the right first call and can direct you from there.

What if I am not sure what animal I have?

Start with the diagnostic guide rather than a guess. Sound, timing and droppings identify most attic tenants from your side of the ceiling, and the guide's tables do the matching. If the clues conflict, an inspection settles it for $150-$300 and produces the entry-point map every fix needs anyway. Calling for the wrong animal costs nothing here, because the first visit is an inspection either way; calling a firm that sells one species can cost a mis-sold job.

Can I get help for a second home or a rental?

Yes, both have their own pages here because the problems differ from an occupied family home. Second homes need vacant-winter protection and a spring opening check; rentals need clear lines on who calls, who pays and what documentation the owner receives. The methods under them are the same. Start on the second-home or landlords page and the inspection will be planned around access, distance and paperwork from the first call.
