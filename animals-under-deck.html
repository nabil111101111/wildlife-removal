Animals Under Decks, Porches and Sheds

The space under a deck is a ready-made den: dry, dark, sheltered and close to food. Skunks and groundhogs move in first, then raccoons and opossums follow. Evicting them is easy. Keeping them out takes buried mesh and an L-footer, and this page shows how that barrier works.

Who lives under there, and how you know

Skunks den under porches and decks from late winter, and a mother raises kits there through spring, which is when the smell risk peaks and patience matters most. Groundhogs dig real burrows under sheds and deck footings, with a main entrance, a plunge hole for escapes and mounds of pale soil as the giveaway. Opossums are tenants rather than diggers, moving into burrows others made. Raccoons use the space as a daytime rest site, especially near water or food. The signs separate them: skunk scent and shallow dig pits in the lawn, groundhog soil mounds and gnawed wood, or the comings and goings at dusk that a trail camera or a flour patch at the entrance settles in one night.

Timing shapes the whole job. A den occupied in March may hold skunk kits by April and groundhog young by May. Eviction then follows the mother-and-babies protocol used on the humane standard page: one-way doors fitted so the family leaves together, a waiting period, then the barrier. Sealing a den with young inside is the one outcome every step here is designed to avoid, and it is why a spring job starts with questions, not with mesh.

  • Soil mounds and a wide main burrow entrance: groundhog
  • Shallow dig pits in lawn and a faint skunk scent: skunk
  • Dusk departures from one gap under the skirt: opossum or raccoon
  • Flour patch at the entrance shows tracks by morning

The L-footer: the barrier that actually works

Surface fixes fail because these animals dig. A board along the deck skirt buys a week. The working barrier is a mesh apron: galvanised or stainless hardware cloth fixed to the deck or porch frame, run down below ground level, then bent outward at ninety degrees into an L-footer that lies buried under the soil pointing away from the structure. An animal digging at the base meets mesh, moves outward to try again, and meets the flat footer it cannot think its way under. The trench is backfilled, and the barrier disappears from view while it works.

The same detail protects sheds on grade, porch skirts and the edges of crawlspaces. Gates and access panels are the weak points and need mesh behind them too. Corners must be folded and joined, not butted, because a groundhog tests corners first. Ask any operator fitting a barrier to show you the footer detail, in a photo of a past job or a sketch, before you agree. The footer is the whole idea; mesh that stops at ground level is a fence a digger treats as a starting line.

One-way doors and the order of work

Eviction comes before the barrier, using one-way doors fitted over the active entrances while secondary holes are monitored. The animals leave at dusk to feed and cannot return, and they relocate themselves to a backup den, which skunks and groundhogs almost always keep. After several quiet nights and a flour or camera check, the doors come off and the buried mesh goes in along every side, not only the side the animal used. Anything else trains the next tenant to try the unprotected side first.

Why this problem rarely fixes itself

Homeowners often wait a week or two, hoping the animal moves on. Sometimes a wandering male does leave. A denning mother, a bat colony or a squirrel with a stored food cache usually stays, and the damage grows while you wait: more droppings, more trampled insulation, more chewed material, and a stronger scent trail that draws other animals later. In upstate New York the October to March attic-entry push makes waiting riskier still, because a settled animal in autumn is planning to stay for winter. Early inspection costs little compared with cleanup after a full season of occupation.

How deck exclusion is fitted

1. Confirm who is home

Identify the species and check for young with tracks, cameras and seasonal timing before any door is fitted.

2. Fit one-way doors

Mount doors over active entrances and monitor secondary holes so the whole family can leave together.

3. Wait and verify

Allow several nights, then confirm the den is empty with a flour patch, camera or door check.

4. Dig the trench

Open a trench along each side of the deck, porch or shed to below the digging line.

5. Fit mesh with an L-footer

Fix hardware cloth to the frame, run it down the trench and bend the footer outward before backfilling.

6. Finish the weak points

Mesh gates, corners and access panels, then walk the whole barrier line with the homeowner.

Frequently asked questions about animals under decks

How do I keep skunks from denning under my porch?

Evict first, with a one-way door outside the kit season or after the young are mobile, then fit buried mesh with an L-footer along the porch edges. Remove the welcome mat as well: secure bins, bring pet food in and clear fallen fruit. A skunk chooses your porch for shelter plus food nearby. Take away both and the porch stays empty even before the barrier proves itself. What is an L-footer on a wildlife barrier?

An L-footer is the underground bend at the bottom of a buried mesh barrier. The mesh runs down from the deck or shed, then turns ninety degrees outward, lying flat under the soil away from the structure. A digging animal works down at the base, hits the mesh, backs out and tries further from the wall, where the flat footer still blocks it. That bend is what separates a barrier from a screen. How much does it cost to animal-proof a deck?

Deck proofing follows the size of the barrier run and sits on top of the usual removal ranges: inspection $150-$300 and single-animal removal $300-$600. A short porch side is at the low end of quotes, a full deck and shed run higher, and cleanup is quoted after inspection where a latrine or carcass is involved. The quote should price the footer detail by the metre of trench, not as a lump sum with no method behind it. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

There are baby skunks under my deck. What should I do?

Wait, if you safely can, and call for a plan rather than a quick fix. Skunk kits stay in the den for their first weeks, then follow their mother out. A one-way door fitted at the right stage lets the family leave together. Sealing or gassing a den with kits inside leaves dead animals under your porch and a far worse problem. An operator will time the door to the kits age and check the den is empty before any mesh goes in. Can I use a one-way door for a groundhog under my shed?

Groundhogs are usually evicted by a combination of one-way doors on the main entrance and monitored secondary holes, fitted outside the season when young are below ground. They are strong diggers, so every entrance must be accounted for, including the plunge hole hidden in grass or under a fence line. Once the burrow is confirmed empty, the L-footer barrier goes in along the shed edges, because a groundhog that dug one burrow will happily dig the next one beside it. Why does something keep digging under my foundation?

Because the soil there is dry, sheltered and easy, and because a previous tenant scent-marked it as a proven den. Each occupation makes the spot more attractive to the next animal. Surface repairs fail for the same reason each year. The lasting fix is the buried barrier with a footer, fitted after the current tenant is out, plus removal of food sources nearby. Anything less is a subscription to the same problem every spring. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Will mothballs or ammonia keep animals out from under the deck?

No, not reliably, and mothballs are a poor idea anywhere near living space: they are a pesticide product, they are not labelled for this use, and animals habituate or simply den beside them. Ammonia rags fade within days. These tricks waste the one resource that matters in kit season, which is time. Spend the same week on a one-way door and a barrier quote and the problem ends instead of pausing. How do I know the den under my deck is empty?

Use three checks together: a one-way door that has shown exits and no returns, a flour or sand patch at the entrance that stays track-free for several nights, and no sound, smell or sighting in the same period. A trail camera settles arguments cheaply. Only when all three agree does the trench get dug. Operators who seal on one quiet night are gambling with young animals underground, and the homeowner inherits the loss. Do groundhogs damage foundations?

A burrow under a footing or slab removes the soil that supports it, and over seasons that can mean settling, cracked slabs and water finding new paths into a basement or crawlspace. The risk grows with the size of the burrow system and how close it runs to the structure. It is another reason to treat a groundhog under a shed or deck as a this-season job: the animal is easy to evict, and the soil it moves does not move back. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Can I fit buried mesh myself?

A fit homeowner with a spade can fit short runs, and the method on this page is the whole recipe: hardware cloth, fixed to the frame, down a trench, footer bent outward, corners folded. The parts people skip are the one-way door stage and the empty-den checks, and those are the parts that protect young animals and your conscience. If the den is occupied, or kits are possible, bring an operator in for the eviction and fit mesh afterwards if you want the saving.
