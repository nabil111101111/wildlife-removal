Dead Animal Removal and Odour Control

A dead animal in a wall or attic announces itself by smell, and the smell peaks just as you hope it will fade. Finding the carcass is the skilled part of this job. This page covers where animals die in houses, how removal works, and what actually ends the odour.

Prevention after a carcass: reading the lesson

Every dead animal indoors is a message about the building, and the follow-up visit should read it aloud. The species and the state of the remains say how long the route was open. The place it died maps the void it travelled. A mouse in a wall usually means a quarter-inch gap at a pipe entry. A squirrel in a soffit means a roofline point that live squirrels are probably still using. A raccoon in an attic means the live removal conversation is not finished. Operators close the job by saying which of those your case is and what sealing answers it, with the entry point photographed. Homeowners who skip that reading tend to meet the same lesson again, one winter later, in a worse smell.

Where dead animals are usually found

Wall voids top the list. An animal that falls into a wall cavity from the attic often cannot climb back up smooth framing, especially mice, squirrels and young raccoons. Attics come second, usually in a corner, under insulation or inside a soffit. Crawlspaces, chimneys and the space under a deck account for most of the rest. The strongest smell point inside the house is a clue, but it misleads: odour travels along pipes, ducts and wiring runs, so the carcass is often a metre or two from the worst room.

Flies are the second clue. A sudden round of large flies at one window, or larvae near a baseboard, points to a carcass close by. Operators work from these signs plus access points: opening a small, planned section of wall or lifting insulation in a mapped area beats the random holes frustrated homeowners sometimes cut. Expect the operator to explain the search plan and any opening needed before anything is cut.

How the odour timeline works

The smell from a dead animal follows a rough curve. It starts within a day or two in warm weather, longer in cold. It peaks as decomposition is most active, often around days four to ten for a small animal, later for a raccoon-sized one. Then it fades over one to three weeks as the remains dry. Heat speeds everything up, which is why a carcass in a sun-warmed wall in July is a crisis and the same problem in January moves slowly.

That timeline explains the honest advice you will hear: a tiny mouse in an unreachable spot may be left to dry out with odour treatment, while a squirrel, skunk or raccoon should come out. Size decides. Removing the source ends the smell at its cause. Everything else, sprays, ozone, candles, only manages the symptom while nature finishes the job on its own schedule.

After removal: sealing so it does not repeat

A dead animal indoors means an animal got indoors. Once the carcass is out and the area is treated, the entry point that admitted it needs the same exclusion work as a live removal: screening, flashing and sealing matched to the species. Skipping that step is how houses collect a second carcass the following winter. The operator should also check for a live remainder: where one animal died, a mate or young may still be present, and that changes the job from removal of a carcass to humane removal as well.

How this service fits the wider job

Removal on its own rarely ends a wildlife problem in upstate New York. Animals follow scent trails, roof gaps and easy food, and a house that suited one raccoon or squirrel will suit the next one. That is why dead animal removal sits inside a wider sequence: inspect the whole roofline and attic, remove the animals that are present, seal every entry point with materials animals cannot chew through, then clean up what was left behind. Skip a step and the odds of a repeat visit climb fast. Homeowners in Ithaca, Binghamton, Poughkeepsie and the other towns we cover see the same pattern each winter, when cold weather pushes animals toward warm attic space between October and March.

What a proper inspection checks first

A proper inspection for dead animal removal starts outside the house, not inside it. The operator walks the roofline, soffits, gable vents, chimney flashing and foundation, looking for gaps of a quarter inch or more, rub marks, droppings and nesting material. Inside the attic, the check covers droppings, trails through insulation, chewed materials and signs of young animals. Findings should be photographed and explained in plain language, with entry points marked on a simple list you keep. If a quote arrives before anyone has looked in the attic, treat it as a guess rather than an estimate.

Frequently asked questions about dead animal removal

How long does dead animal smell last if I leave it?

A mouse may fade within one to two weeks. A squirrel or skunk can smell strongly for three to six weeks, and a raccoon longer, especially in warm weather. The smell peaks partway through, then eases as remains dry out. Leaving it is only sensible for very small animals in truly unreachable spots. Anything larger is worth removing, because the timeline is long and the odour soaks into insulation and timber. Who removes dead animals from attics and walls?

A licensed nuisance wildlife control operator is the right call for a carcass inside a structure. Municipal animal control generally handles animals on public land and live domestic animals, not wildlife inside your walls. Operators have the cameras, respirators and access tools to find and remove the source, treat the area, and seal the entry point that let the animal in originally. Can you find a dead animal without cutting my walls?

Often, yes. Operators use the smell pattern, fly activity, attic checks and inspection cameras through small existing gaps to narrow the spot first. Sometimes a planned opening is the only route, and a careful operator cuts one clean section that patches easily rather than several test holes. You should be told where any opening is proposed and why, and approve it before cutting starts. What should I do if an animal died in my attic?

Keep people and pets out of the attic and do not handle the carcass yourself. Note where the smell is strongest and when it started, and check whether you still hear any live noises, which would mean other animals remain. Then book a removal visit. The operator will remove the source, treat the area, check insulation and identify the entry point so the same route can be sealed. Is a dead animal in the house a health risk?

The main concerns are bacteria, parasites left behind and the fluids that soak into insulation and timber as decomposition runs its course. Risk to people is managed by distance and proper removal: gloves, respirators and bagging by the operator, and treatment of the surface afterwards. For personal health questions, consult health professionals. For the house, prompt removal and cleanup of the affected zone is the practical answer. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Why does the smell get worse before it gets better?

Decomposition is most active in its middle stage, and that is when gas and odour peak. Warmth accelerates it, so a carcass in a heated wall smells worse on warm days and in the afternoon. This peak fools people into thinking a new animal has died. It is usually the same one, further along. Once remains dry, the smell drops quickly, which is the pattern operators use to judge how long a carcass has been there. Will removing the carcass remove the smell completely?

Removing the carcass removes the source, and the smell then fades as the area is treated and aired. Where fluids have soaked into insulation or timber, that material may need to come out or be treated in place for the odour to clear fully. A straight operator will flag that at the visit rather than promising a perfect result from removal alone. Persistent smell after removal usually means a second source or soaked material remains. If you are unsure, start with an inspection and written findings, then decide on the next step with clear information in hand.

Can a dead skunk under my deck be removed?

Yes. Dead skunks under decks and porches are a common call, and the work combines crawlspace access, bagging and odour treatment of the soil or boards the animal lay on. The den entrance should then be screened with buried mesh or an L-footer barrier once any live animals are confirmed out, so the shelter does not refill. Expect the smell in the yard to ease over days once the source is gone. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.
