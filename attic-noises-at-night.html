Attic Noises at Night: Scratching, Scurrying and Thumping

Attic noise feels louder at 2 a.m. because the house is quiet and you are lying still, listening. The good news: the hour is the best clue you have. Night noise rules out the day animals and narrows the list fast. This page walks the sounds one by one.

The night shift, animal by animal

Flying squirrels open the night. They glide in after dark, land with a soft thud on the roof or in the attic, and move with a light, rapid pitter-patter. Colonies make it busier: several animals, quick bursts, then quiet spells. Mice and rats run fixed paths, so their scurrying repeats along the same line, often inside a wall as well as across the ceiling, with gnawing pauses at the same spot. Rats are heavier and slower than mice, and their runs sometimes include a dragging sound.

Raccoons are the heavy end of the scale. Their steps thump, their movement is slow and deliberate, and spring litters add chattering, chirring and the scuffle of young animals wrestling. Bats barely register as travel noise at all. Listen instead at the roost: soft scratching, faint squeaks and fluttering at dusk as the colony leaves, and again before dawn. A sound that matches none of these, rhythmic tapping or a hum that runs with heating cycles, may be the house itself, pipes or ducts expanding, and that is worth ruling out too.

  • Pitter-patter after dark: flying squirrels
  • Scurrying on the same path nightly: mice or rats
  • Heavy thumps and chatter: raccoon family
  • Flutter at dusk and dawn in one spot: bats

Why winter nights are the loudest

New York winters push animals deep into attic life. The October to March entry push fills attics in autumn, and by January the residents are settled, feeding from stored caches and moving through insulation that carries sound straight to the ceiling boards. Cold also shrinks timber and opens small gaps, so a house can gain a new entry point mid-winter after ice works at a vent or fascia board. Noise that starts suddenly after a freeze or a storm deserves an outside look at the roofline once daylight and safe conditions allow.

Babies change the soundtrack by season. Raccoon kits from March to June add voices. Squirrel litters, December to February and May to June, add light scampering in the day as the young start moving. A noise diary that notes the date as well as the hour helps an operator read these patterns and time any removal around young animals correctly rather than discovering them mid-job.

What not to do at 2 a.m.

Do not climb into the attic at night to investigate. You risk a fall, a cornered animal and a bite, and you will learn less than your ears already told you. Do not bang on the ceiling under a den with young: a startled mother moves her litter deeper into the structure, sometimes into wall voids. Do not block the outside hole in the morning before help arrives. A trapped animal chews out through whatever is nearest, and that is often a ceiling, a wall or a bundle of wiring.

What helps instead is boring and useful. Log the hour, the room and the sound for two or three nights. Take a phone photo of any droppings visible from the hatch in daylight, and of any roofline damage visible from the ground. Sleep in another room if you need to. Then make one call with real information in hand, which shortens the inspection and usually the bill that follows it.

Frequently asked questions about attic noises

What animal makes scratching noises in the attic at night?

At night, scratching usually comes from mice, rats or flying squirrels. Grey squirrels sleep at night, so their name drops off the list after dark. Light scratching that moves fast and changes place fits flying squirrels. Scratching tied to scurrying along the same route fits rodents. Bats scratch softly in one spot near the roost, mostly around dusk and dawn, and add faint fluttering that rodents never make. Why do I only hear the noises at night?

Two reasons meet at night. The active species are nocturnal, so their working hours start when yours end. And the house is quiet, the street is quiet and you are lying still under the ceiling, so sounds that daytime noise covers become plain. Day-active grey squirrels are the mirror case: homeowners at work all day sometimes only learn of them from droppings, because the household is out during squirrel hours. Will the animal in my attic leave on its own?

A single wandering animal sometimes leaves within days. A settled one does not: it has shelter, warmth and often food or young in your attic. Night noise that repeats at the same hours for a week is a resident, not a visitor. Waiting while it settles costs more in droppings, chewing and scent marking than an early inspection, and it gives litters time to arrive in spring. Are attic noises worse in winter in New York?

Yes, in most houses. Animals enter during the October to March push and settle for the season, moving through insulation directly above heated rooms. Stored food caches, winter litters of squirrels and the simple fact of a full house of people indoors listening all add to it. Noise that began with the first freeze is a classic sign of an autumn entry point that an inspection will find on the roofline. What does a raccoon in the attic sound like at night?

A raccoon sounds heavier than people expect, like a small person shifting weight: slow thumps, deliberate steps and sometimes a dragging noise as it moves nesting material. With kits, from March onward, add chattering, chirring and muffled scuffles. The noise stops by day when the family sleeps. If the sound could never be a mouse, trust that instinct and plan for a raccoon inspection with a babies check. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Can bats be the source of scratching in my ceiling?

Yes, if the scratching stays in one place near the ridge or a gable and peaks at dusk and dawn. Bats scratch and shuffle as they cluster, and their wings add a soft flutter rodents cannot copy. Check for guano below that spot from the hatch. Bat work in New York follows blackout windows, so an early inspection matters: it confirms the colony and books exclusion for the first lawful window. I hear gnawing in the walls at night. What gnaws?

Mice and rats gnaw in walls at night, keeping their teeth worn and working along fixed paths between the attic and food below. Squirrels gnaw too, but by day and usually in the attic itself. Persistent gnawing at one wall spot can mean an animal working on a stud or trying to widen a route. It deserves an inspection because wall voids sit close to wiring runs, and gnawing near cables is the fire-risk case on this site. Should I play loud music or use bright lights to drive animals out?

Harassment sometimes moves an animal a few metres and rarely evicts one. Mothers return to young through noise that would drive off a casual visitor, and settled animals habituate within days. Lights and radios can help confirm activity patterns during an inspection, but they are not a removal method. Worse, a panicked animal may move into a wall void, turning a straightforward attic job into a harder one. What if the noises stop suddenly?

Sudden silence has three common causes. The animal may have left, which is the happy case. It may have died in the structure, in which case a smell follows within days. Or a mother may have moved her young to a quieter part of the building. Watch for a week, note any odour, and have the entry point checked before sealing. Sealing straight after silence risks closing in an animal that is simply quiet. Do attic noises between March and October mean babies?

Often, yes. Raccoon kits arrive from March, squirrel litters run into early summer and again around December to February, and bat pups are born in the maternity season.
