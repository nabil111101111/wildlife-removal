Bat in the Bedroom: What to Do Tonight

Most bats in houses are a nuisance. A bat in a room where someone slept is a health question first and a nuisance second, because a bat bite can be too small to notice. The rule on this page is the one New York health guidance is built on: do not release that bat until you have checked what to do.

Why the sleeping-room rule exists

Rabies in bats is uncommon, and most bats in New York houses are healthy animals in the wrong building. The caution exists because of how the risk works: a bat's teeth are tiny, a bite or scratch can leave no clear mark, and a sleeping person, a small child, or anyone who cannot reliably say what happened may not know contact occurred. Health authorities in New York therefore treat a bat found in a room with a sleeping person, an unattended child, or a person who cannot report contact as a possible exposure until guidance says otherwise. That is a careful rule, not an alarmist one, and following it costs one phone call.

Releasing the bat destroys the one piece of evidence that could settle the question. If the bat is captured and available, your county health department can advise whether testing is appropriate, and a negative result ends the matter. If the bat is gone, the advice has to assume the worse case. So the sequence matters more than speed: contain first, call second, release only when the guidance you receive says the situation allows it. Everything else on this page is detail under that single rule.

  • Bat in a room with a sleeping person: do not release it
  • Bat in a room with an unattended child: do not release it
  • Anyone unsure whether contact happened: treat as possible exposure
  • County health department advises on testing and next steps

Containing and capturing the bat safely

If the bat is still in the room, close the door, put a towel along the gap at the bottom, and keep people and pets out. If it is flying, wait for it to land; bats in houses usually settle high, on a curtain, wall or beam. Capture, when you do it, is a calm job: thick gloves, a rigid container placed over the bat where it rests, stiff card slid underneath, lid secured and taped. Never handle a bat with bare hands and never try to catch one in flight. If capture is beyond you, a licensed operator does it with the right equipment, and tonight's containment is enough until that visit.

If the bat has left, or was released before you read this, do not chase the matter backwards: note who was in the room, when, and anything remembered about contact, and make the health call anyway. Guidance exists for exactly that case and it is a common one. Then turn to the building: a bat in a bedroom arrived from somewhere, often the attic colony above it, and the entry-point inspection and lawful-season exclusion on the bat page are what stop the sequel.

After tonight: proofing the bedroom and the house

While the health question runs its course, the house question can start. Check the bedroom's own gaps first: window screens whole and seated, gaps around window units and chimneys, the attic hatch seal. Then the wider inspection the bat page describes, roost signs in the attic, guano, staining at small gaps along the ridge and gables. Exclusion itself follows the bat calendar, but the inspection does not have to wait, and a house that knows its entry list in June is first in the diary when the August window opens.

General safety rules that apply on every page here

Keep distance from any wild animal, even one that looks calm or injured. Keep children and pets away from droppings, nesting material and carcasses. Do not handle bats, raccoons or skunks with bare hands, and wash hands and tools after any contact with attic or crawlspace material. Photograph from a safe distance if you need a record. Where this page mentions health topics, it gives general information only, drawn from public health guidance, and it points you to health professionals and your county health department for advice about your own situation.

When to stop and get help first

Some situations should not wait for a routine appointment. A bat found in a room with a sleeping person, a child or anyone who cannot say whether they were bitten needs health guidance the same day. An animal acting strangely, staggering or approaching people in daylight should be reported, not approached. A carcass in a living space, or droppings across a large attic area, calls for professional cleanup rather than a household vacuum. Knowing which bucket your problem sits in is most of staying safe.

Frequently asked questions about a bat in a bedroom

I found a bat in a room where my child was sleeping. What do I do?

Keep the bat contained in that room if it is still there, towel under the door, child and pets out, and do not release it. Then contact your county health department or health professionals for guidance today. Because a sleeping child cannot report contact and a bat bite can leave no visible mark, health guidance treats this as a possible exposure until advised otherwise. This page gives general information only; the health department gives the decision for your child. For health questions, consult health professionals or your county health department and follow current NYSDOH guidance for your situation.

Should I catch the bat for testing?

If it can be done safely, yes: capture keeps the option of testing open, and the county health department advises whether testing is needed in your case. Use thick gloves and a rigid container over a landed bat, card underneath, lid secured. Never bare hands, never in flight. If you cannot capture it safely, contain the room and let a licensed operator do it. Either way, the health call comes first in importance and costs nothing. This page gives general information only. Health professionals and your county health department are the right source for personal advice.

What does a bat bite look like?

Often, nothing clear: bat teeth are very small and a bite can feel like a pinprick or leave marks too faint to find, which is the entire reason the sleeping-room rule exists. Do not try to settle the question by inspecting skin, your own or a child's. If there is any chance of contact, health professionals and your county health department judge the exposure from the circumstances. That judgment, not a torch and a hopeful look, is the safety step. When health exposure is possible, check guidance first and act on that guidance rather than on general information from a website.

How do I get a bat out of a bedroom at night?

If nobody slept in the room and contact is not a question, open an exterior window wide, close the doors to the rest of the house, darken the room and wait; most bats leave within the hour. If anyone slept there, the rule changes: contain the bat instead and check health guidance before any release. The distinction feels small at 3 a.m. and it is the whole page: release is fine for a bat in an empty room, not for a bat in a slept-in one. For health questions, consult health professionals or your county health department and follow current NYSDOH guidance for your situation.

Do I need rabies shots if a bat was in my bedroom?

That decision belongs to health professionals and your county health department, made from your circumstances: who was in the room, whether the bat is available, what contact is possible. This page deliberately gives no answer, because a website cannot judge an exposure. What it can say is: make the call promptly, keep the bat contained meanwhile, and do not let embarrassment about waking people delay it. Health departments handle this exact call routinely, including at night. This page gives general information only. Health professionals and your county health department are the right source for personal advice.

How does a bat get into a bedroom in the first place?

Usually from the attic above it: down a wall void, through a gap at the attic hatch, a chimney, or a plumbing run, then out at a light fitting or a closet. Sometimes through the bedroom's own window screen gap or an open flue. A colony in the attic makes bedroom visits far more likely, which is why one bat in a room earns a full inspection rather than a shrug. The entry the bat used is on the list that lawful exclusion then seals. When health exposure is possible, check guidance first and act on that guidance rather than on general information from a website.

What if the bat got away before we could contain it?

Write down what you know while it is fresh: the room, the date and hours, who was there, whether anyone woke with the bat on or near them, and anything else remembered. Then make the health call with that note in hand. This is a common situation and guidance exists for it; the absence of the bat changes the advice you may receive, which is why the note matters. Afterwards, book the inspection so the next bat meets a sealed house. For health questions, consult health professionals or your county health department and follow current NYSDOH guidance for your situation.

Are bats in New York likely to have rabies?

A small share of bats tested in New York are positive, and the bats most often tested are the ones already behaving oddly or found in houses, so the figure overstates the risk in the general bat population. Most bats are healthy and best left alone. The caution in this page is not that every bat is dangerous; it is that the cost of the check is one call while the cost of guessing wrong is high. That arithmetic, not fear, is the policy. This page gives general information only. Health professionals and your county health department are the right source for personal advice.

How do I bat-proof a bedroom?

Start with the room: window screens whole and seated, gaps at window units sealed, the attic hatch fitting close, chimney and flue checked, and gaps at pipe and cable entries closed. Then proof the house, because bedroom bats usually arrive from an attic roost: the full inspection and lawful exclusion on the bat page. A bedroom sealed against a house with an open ridge is a locked room in an open building, so the order is house first in planning, room first in checking tonight.
