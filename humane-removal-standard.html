The no-poison pledge, and why it exists

Poison fails on every count that matters. A poisoned animal rarely dies where you want it to: it dies in a wall, a soffit or a neighbour's yard, and the odour and cleanup follow. Secondary poisoning reaches the pets, owls, hawks and other animals that eat poisoned rodents or carrion. And poison does nothing about the hole, the scent trail or the next tenant. It is refused here without exceptions, including for mice and rats in a structure, where sealing and trapping do the same job without scattering the problem across the food chain.

The same logic refuses glue traps and body-gripping methods inside homes. They are slow, they injure animals that then die hidden, and they catch non-target animals, including the bats and birds this region's houses host every summer. A standard has to name what it excludes, or it is only a promise about the good days. These methods are excluded on matched jobs, in writing, on this page, where any homeowner can quote it back.

  • No poison, in any structure, for any species
  • No glue traps or body-gripping traps in homes
  • One-way doors are the default eviction method
  • Lethal control is not part of matched residential work

The mother-and-babies protocol

From March, most attic calls involve a mother and young. The protocol is fixed. First, locate the litter and age it. Second, fit the one-way door or eviction route so the mother can leave. Third, move the young to a warm reunion box placed by the exit, where the mother collects them and carries them to her backup den. Fourth, confirm the whole family is out before any sealing is fitted. Raccoon mothers almost always relocate their kits themselves when given the route and the chance, and the same pattern serves squirrels and skunks under decks.

What the protocol refuses is just as clear. No sealing a den with young inside. No removing a mother and leaving the litter to starve and die in the structure, which is both cruel and, in odour terms, self-defeating. No rushing a job to beat a calendar when a week of patience finishes it cleanly. Where bat pups are involved, the law adds its own frame: exclusion waits outside the maternity blackout entirely, and the colony is planned around the dates, not argued with.

Relocation: what New York law actually allows

Homeowners often ask for an animal to be trapped and released in a park or woodland. New York tightly restricts that idea. Relocating wildlife is regulated, many species cannot legally be moved by homeowners at all, and moving an animal can spread disease and dumps it in another animal's territory, where survival is poor. Licensed operators work within nuisance wildlife rules that favour resolving the conflict on site: evict the animal from the structure, seal the structure, and let the animal return to its own home range nearby, where it knows the food, water and shelter.

That is better for the animal and better for you. A raccoon evicted from your attic to its own territory stops being your problem the moment the hole is sealed. A raccoon driven ten miles is a frightened animal in a stranger's range, and the research on relocated wildlife survival is grim reading. Humane, in this trade, turns out to be the practical option too: eviction plus exclusion, done once, done properly, with the animal alive and your house closed.

The standard we hold operators to

Every operator matched through this site is expected to hold a current New York nuisance wildlife control operator licence, carry insurance, work humanely without poison, and put findings and prices in writing before work starts. Those are not marketing lines. They are checks a homeowner can repeat in minutes using the steps on this site, and they are the same checks we suggest you run on any company you find elsewhere, including firms a neighbour recommends. A good operator welcomes the questions. An operator who dodges them has told you something useful about how the job will go.

Written findings beat verbal promises

Wildlife work happens in attics, on roofs and under decks, in places most homeowners never see. That makes paperwork matter more here than in most trades. Ask for photographs of entry points before and after sealing, a list of materials used, notes on any young animals found and how they were handled, and the terms of any warranty in writing. Keep the file with your house records. It helps with insurance questions, with a future sale, and with the next operator if a different species turns up two winters from now.

Frequently asked questions about the humane standard

Do you use poison for animals in the attic?

No, never, and the refusal covers mice and rats as well as the larger animals. Poisoned animals die in walls and soffits, secondary poisoning reaches pets and birds of prey, and not one entry point gets sealed by it. The matched method is eviction or trapping plus metal exclusion, which solves the animal and the access in the same job. If a quote from any firm leans on poison for an attic problem, that quote is solving a different, smaller problem than the one you have. What if there are baby animals involved?

Then the job follows the mother-and-babies protocol: find and age the litter, open an eviction route for the mother, place the young in a reunion box by the exit so she collects them, and seal only when the family is confirmed out together. It adds days, not weeks, in most cases. Separating a mother from her young is refused on matched jobs, for welfare reasons and because dead young in a wall are a worse outcome for the homeowner too. Is trapping and relocating wildlife legal in New York?

For homeowners, generally no for most species, and for operators only within the nuisance wildlife rules and permits that govern the work. Relocation is restricted because it spreads disease and because relocated animals survive poorly in unfamiliar territory. The lawful, standard approach is on-site resolution: evict the animal from the structure, seal the entries, and let it return to its own range. An operator offering casual relocation as the main method should be asked under what authority it is done. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Will the animals suffer during humane removal?

The methods are chosen to keep stress short and injury out of the picture. One-way doors let an animal leave under its own power at its normal hour. Reunion boxes keep young warm and where the mother expects to find them. Handling, where it happens, is brief and by licensed operators. No method is stress-free, and this page will not pretend otherwise, but the contrast with poison, glue traps and sealed-in litters is the whole reason the standard exists. Why not just shoot or kill the animal and be done?

Because it does not finish the job and it is rarely lawful inside a residential structure. A dead animal leaves the hole open, the scent trail fresh and the young, in season, still in the attic. Lethal control also carries legal limits by species and place that homeowners often do not know they are crossing. Eviction plus exclusion ends the problem permanently with the animal alive elsewhere, which is why it is the standard method rather than a soft-hearted alternative. How do one-way doors work humanely?

A one-way door is a simple flap or funnel fitted over the entry point. The animal pushes out as normal at dusk or dawn and the door closes behind it; returning, it cannot push in against the flap. The animal is never handled and leaves at its own pace. Doors stay on for several nights while activity is monitored, then come off as the point is sealed in metal. For bats the same idea uses exclusion tubes or netting suited to their size and flight. That approach keeps the work humane, avoids sealing an animal inside, and gives you a record of exactly what was done.

Are the methods safe around children and pets?

Yes, and they are chosen with households in mind. No poison is laid anywhere a child or pet could reach, because no poison is used at all. Traps, where fitted, are placed in attics, on rooflines or inside dens, out of living space, and the operator will tell you which areas to keep pets from while doors are on. Tell the operator about free-roaming cats and dogs at inspection, because eviction timing can be planned around them. What happens to the animal after it is evicted?

It returns to its home range, which for most of these species is a few streets or fields wide, and it uses a backup den. Skunks, raccoons and groundhogs all keep alternates. Your house leaves its list because the entries are sealed and the scent is cleaned. The animal's life continues where it already knew the food and shelter, which is exactly why eviction outperforms relocation for survival as well as for your roofline. Does humane removal cost more than lethal control?

On the standard ranges here, no: inspection $150-$300, a single-animal humane removal $300-$600, bat exclusion $800-$2,500. Lethal callouts can quote lower because they skip the exclusion half of the job, and the saving is repaid at the next occupation. Humane removal with sealing is the complete job priced once.
