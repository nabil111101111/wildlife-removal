How the checker decides: the four clues

Sound is the strongest clue

Weight and rhythm separate almost everything. A raccoon is the only common attic animal heavy enough to thump and drag. Squirrels roll nuts and debris, a sound like marbles on a board. Mice scratch lightly and repetitively. Bats barely scratch at all; what you hear is fluttering at the roost and movement at dusk. And a problem with no sound at all, only smell, is rarely an attic problem: it is a skunk den under the structure, or something that has died in a void.

Timing rules out half the list

Squirrels keep daylight hours and are loudest at dawn and dusk as they leave and return. Raccoons, opossums, mice and bats are night animals, but bats compress their noise into the dusk departure and dawn return. A sound that runs all night at a steady level is usually mice on their runs. Daytime chirping from a chimney in summer is nestlings being fed, which names swifts and roughly dates the nest.

Location points at the system, not just the species

Chimney sounds mean swifts, or a raccoon using the flue as a den. Wall sounds mean void travellers, mice above all, climbing from a foundation gap to the attic. Under-deck problems are ground animals, skunks, groundhogs and opossums, and the fix is screening and barriers, not attic work. Naming the location correctly is what stops people paying for the wrong trade.

Signs confirm what the ears suggest

Droppings are the signature: rice-sized and pointed for mice, blunt and olive-sized for rats, crumbling pellets for bats, finger-length and gathered in latrines for raccoons. Entry holes talk too: a clean chewed edge at a vent is squirrel work, a pried and bent opening is a raccoon's, and a quarter-inch gap that looks like nothing at all is exactly what a mouse needs. The checker weighs your signs against your sounds; where they conflict, believe the signs.

If the answer is a bat in a bedroom, stop here

One result outranks the tool. If a bat has been in a room where someone was sleeping, or in a room with a child or anyone who cannot reliably say whether they were touched, do not catch and release it, and do not treat the evening as a simple removal job. Keep the room closed and contact your county health department for guidance before anything else, because the bat may need to be available for testing. This site gives no medical advice. The full protocol is on the bat in the bedroom page.

Once you know what it is

The method from there is the same for every animal: inspect and map every entry point, check for young before anything is sealed, evict humanely with one-way doors or live capture, then seal, repair and clean up. What changes by species is timing and hardware. Bats carry two legal blackout windows, May 1 to August 15 and October 1 to March 31. Swift nests wait for fledging. Spring litters of raccoons, squirrels and skunks move as families or not at all. Costs sit in the same canonical bands whichever animal the checker named: inspection $150 to $300, single-animal removal $300 to $600, bat exclusion $800 to $2,500, and cleanup quoted after inspection. Your town page, linked from your checker result, carries the local version: the records for your area, the housing stock the animals are working on, and the operators' process in your county.

Attic animal checker: questions and answers

Does the checker work without JavaScript?

Yes. The interactive checker needs JavaScript, but the table on this page covers the same ground in print: sound, timing and location matched against the eight animals that use upstate New York homes. If the table and your answers disagree, trust the droppings and the entry points over either one, and let the inspection settle it.

How accurate is the attic animal checker?

It narrows most cases to one animal, or two close ones, because sound and timing are genuinely discriminating: only raccoons thump, only squirrels keep daylight hours, only bats flutter at the roofline at dusk. What it cannot do is see your attic. Dropping size, tracks in dust and the shape of the entry hole are the confirming evidence, which is why the result always points to an inspection before any quote.

What if two animals match my answers?

That is normal, and it usually means one of two real situations: two species are present, a squirrel in the eaves and mice in the walls is a classic pairing, or one animal is using another's entry and leaving mixed signs, which is the opossum pattern. An inspection separates them by droppings, tracks and which entries are active. The sealing list then covers both, which is the part that actually ends the problem.

I hear noises in the attic but there are no droppings. What does that mean?

It usually means early, or tidy. A new arrival may not have established a latrine or runs yet, droppings may be falling into wall voids or deep insulation where you cannot see them, and some animals, groundhogs and denning skunks, are not in the attic at all, so their signs are at ground level. Absence of droppings narrows nothing. Work from sound and timing, and check the entry points from outside with binoculars before concluding anything.

The checker says bats, and one was in my bedroom. What should I do?

Stop, and treat it as a health question before a wildlife one. Do not catch and release the bat: if anyone slept in the room, or a child was present who cannot say whether they were touched, the bat may need to be tested, and releasing it removes that option. Keep the room closed, keep people and pets out, and contact your county health department for guidance. This site gives no medical advice; the health department's protocol is the one to follow, and our bat in the bedroom page walks through it step by step.

Will the animal in my attic leave on its own?

Almost never on a useful timescale. Attic animals are there because the space works: warm, dry, safe from predators, close to food. They settle, widen the entry, and breed in season. The genuine exceptions are seasonal departures, swifts leaving in autumn, young dispersing in late summer, and even those leave the open entry behind for next year's tenant. Leaving is not the same as solved.

How much does removal cost once I know the animal?

The canonical bands on this site apply to every species: inspection $150 to $300, single-animal removal $300 to $600, bat exclusion $800 to $2,500, and cleanup quoted after inspection. A single squirrel, raccoon or opossum eviction with one entry sealed sits in the single-animal band. A bat colony is exclusion work. Mice are priced by the length of the sealing list. The written estimate after inspection is the number to trust, with removal, sealing and cleanup as separate lines.

What should I do tonight, before any visit?

Five things. Keep people and pets out of the space and close any interior hatch or door to it. Do not seal any hole from outside: you may trap the animal, or its young, inside. Do not handle the animal or its droppings. Write down what you hear, where, and at what times, for two or three nights. And if the animal is in the living space, or a bat was in a bedroom, skip the list and make the urgent call, or the health department call, tonight.
